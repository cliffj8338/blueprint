What the Police Do All Day

If crime is down, why are there so many calls? Because most calls have nothing to do with crime.

89.8% of Calls Are Not About Crime

29,220 calls in 2025 — here is where they go

In 2025, the department answered 29,220 calls. Of those, 26,241 (89.8%) were classified as non-crime after the officer arrived. Medical emergencies, welfare checks, 911 hangups, building checks, domestic disputes, false alarms, accident investigations. The police department is the borough's only 24/7 service that goes anywhere and refuses no one.

Staffing & Workload

Same officers, more calls. Something had to give.

46 Officers, 21% More Calls

What happens when demand rises but staffing doesn't

In 2015, each officer handled 524 calls. In 2025, that number is 635. The crime workload per officer actually dropped (85 to 65 crimes per officer) because crime fell. But the non-crime workload per officer jumped 30% (439 to 570). The net result: every officer handles 21% more work, and almost all of the new work is community service — not crime.

What Gave: Officer-Initiated Activity

When officers spend more time answering calls, they have less time for proactive work. Traffic stops, DUI patrols, and drug interdiction all happen during the hours between dispatched calls. As those hours shrank, so did the numbers.

18 DUI arrests in 2025 for a borough of 23,400 people. That is a problem. In 2015, officers made 107 DUI arrests. The drop is not because there are fewer drunk drivers — it is because officers are spending more time answering calls and have less time for proactive enforcement.

The one exception is parking citations, which rose 189%. Parking enforcement happens on foot during fixed patrol beats and between calls — it does not need the extended uninterrupted time that traffic stops and DUI patrols require.

Why Arrests Dropped

Total arrests fell from 2,687 to 1,789 (down 898). Two categories explain 316 of that drop: drug arrests (-227, reflecting PA's statewide shift toward treatment) and DUI arrests (-89, reflecting less time for proactive patrols). The remaining 582-arrest reduction tracks the 930-crime reduction — fewer crimes means fewer arrests.

How 46 Officers Become 4 Per Shift

46 officers are divided across 4 squads of about 11-12 each. Each squad has a sergeant, and officers rotate through specialized roles (detectives, K-9, school resource). After FMLA, sick leave, vacation, court, and training, 4-6 officers are available for patrol on any given shift. The department is budgeted for 48 but has never reached it.

What the Numbers Mean

Pottstown's $439 per resident is high compared to the PA average ($285-290) but comparable to Norristown ($456). Both are legacy boroughs with higher poverty rates and service demands than the average PA municipality.

Phoenixville has half the poverty rate and spends $76 less per resident on police. Same industrial heritage, very different economics. Poverty at 6.3% vs Pottstown's 17.5%. Median income of $102,000 vs $61,000.

The poverty-crime connection is real. Higher poverty means more calls, more calls means more cost. Across every borough in this comparison, lower poverty corresponds to lower policing costs.

Pottstown's 1.97 officers per 1,000 residents is at the top of the nationally recommended range (1.5-2.0). Coatesville is higher at 2.24.

59.8% of the general fund goes to police. That is very high. The national average for municipalities is 25-35%. This does not mean the money is wasted — it means policing dominates Pottstown's budget in a way that limits spending on everything else.

Straight Talk

What is working and what is not. No spin.

What's Working and What's Not

An honest assessment based on the numbers

What's Working

  • Overall crime at historic lows. Part I crime fell 30.5% over the decade. Total crimes (Part I + Part II) hit their lowest recorded level in 2025.
  • Violent crime trending down in 2026. Robbery on pace at -47%. Assault at -23%. These are real, sustained improvements.
  • Call volume growth slowing. The year-over-year gap shrank from +8% in January to +1.19% by October.
  • Motor vehicle theft correcting. Down 50% in 2026 as the regional Hyundai/Kia wave subsides.
  • Budget transparency is excellent. Every line item is public. This report verified all 60+ items against the adopted budget.

What's Not Working

  • Theft is up 35%. It is the main Part I driver. Retail theft and theft from cars are the biggest subcategories. This is a real problem, not a statistical blip.
  • DUI enforcement has essentially stopped. 107 arrests in 2015. 18 in 2025. Officers do not have time for traffic enforcement because they are answering calls.
  • The police budget is 59.8% of the general fund. That is high by any standard. The question is not whether the money is well-spent — it is whether the borough can sustain this share long-term.
  • Staffing has been budgeted for 48 officers but has never reached it. The department has been at 44-47 for the entire decade. The report does not answer why, and it should.

Common Questions

"Crime is down. Can we reduce the police budget?"

Crime is down 30.5% but calls for service are up 21.2%. The budget cannot be cut proportionally to crime because the actual workload (calls) went the other direction. The department handles 1,249 calls per 1,000 residents — each one requires an officer response.

"90% of calls are non-crime. Why send sworn officers?"

"Non-crime" describes the outcome, not the response requirement at dispatch. A 911 hangup could be an emergency. A welfare check could uncover a crime. Domestic disturbances are among the most dangerous calls. PA law requires sworn officers for the majority of these calls. Realistically, about 10-15% of non-crime calls could be diverted to civilian responders — if that infrastructure existed. Right now it does not.

"Other boroughs spend less and have lower crime."

Phoenixville spends $76 less per resident, yes. But Phoenixville has 6.3% poverty vs Pottstown's 17.5%, median income of $102,000 vs $61,000, and home values of $370,000 vs $195,000. Crime rates track economic conditions across every peer borough in this comparison. Pottstown's rate has fallen 30.5% over the decade — but economics set the floor.

"Arrests are down 33.4%. Are officers not enforcing the law?"

Drug arrests dropped 227 (PA shifted toward treatment over arrest). DUI arrests dropped 89 (less time for proactive patrols). Those two categories explain 35% of the arrest decline. The rest tracks the crime decline: fewer crimes, fewer arrests.

A Note on Cost Calculations

Two ways to look at the same budget

A separate analysis of Pottstown police calls used a figure of $85.15 per call-hour. That number came from the Chief of Police and covers four cost components: officer hourly rate ($57.97), fuel ($0.15/mi), vehicle cost ($15/hr), and healthcare ($12/hr). It is a reasonable estimate of what it costs to send one car to one call. But it covers only about 24% of the total department cost. The full budget ($10,264,691) includes 60+ line items: overtime, longevity, workers comp, retiree health, civilian staff, vehicles, equipment, training, and capital purchases.

The police budget is a fixed annual cost. The same 46 officers, vehicles, equipment, and benefits cost $10,264,691 whether the department responds to 25,000 calls or 30,000 calls. Calls for service consume about 30.5% of officer time. The rest goes to patrol, investigations, court, training, and administration. Dividing the full budget by calls alone overstates the cost per call, because calls are only one part of what officers do.

The ward-level call data from the alternative analysis is a useful contribution — understanding which wards generate the most calls, and what types, is valuable for resource planning.
