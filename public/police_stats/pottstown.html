Crime reduction tracks economic development

Every major crime category declined over the past decade. During the same period, Census ACS data shows improvements in median income, home values, and poverty rates in the borough. Anticipated Council Critiques

Seven likely challenges and the data that addresses each one
Front-Porch Summary
These six critiques represent the most common objections raised in council discussions. Each one has a specific, data-backed response grounded in the UCR dataset. None of these critiques are unreasonable. All of them have verifiable answers.

Critique 1: "Arrests are down 33.4%. Police aren't doing their jobs."

"Crime dropped 23.8% but arrests dropped 33.4%. That gap proves officers stopped enforcing the law."

Response

Drug arrests dropped 227 (476 to 249). DUI arrests dropped 89 (107 to 18). These two categories account for 316 of the 898 total arrest decline (35.2%). The remaining 582-arrest decline is proportional to the 930-crime decline in total offenses.

Critique 2: "1.97 officers per 1,000 is fine. We don't need more."

"Our ratio is adequate for a borough this size."

Response

The officer-to-resident ratio does not capture workload. Each officer handled 635 calls in 2025 (up 21% from 524 in 2015). 4 officers working per shift process approximately 40 calls per 12-hour shift (~10 per officer, est.). Reaching the budgeted 48 would improve working strength per shift, reducing per-officer dispatch load.

Critique 3: "Traffic citations are down 56.5%. Officers stopped writing tickets."

"DUI arrests dropped 83%. Where are the traffic patrols?"

Response

Traffic citations: 3,438 in 2015, 1,496 in 2025. DUI arrests: 107 in 2015, 18 in 2025. During the same period, total CFS increased 21.2% (24,118 to 29,220) with the same headcount. Estimated reactive CFS response: ~66% of shift time in 2015, ~81% in 2025. May 2026 traffic citations: 126 vs. 89 in May 2025 (+41.6%).

Critique 4: "Crime is down. We should cut the police budget."

"If crime dropped 30%, we can reduce spending proportionally."

Response

Crime is down but total demand is up 21.2% (24,118 to 29,220 CFS). Pottstown spends $252 per resident on policing. Phoenixville spends $350. Norristown spends $456. Pottstown already operates at the lowest per-resident cost among its peers.

Critique 5: "We spend $5.9M and still have 45.8 crimes per 1,000."

"Other boroughs spend more and have lower crime rates."

Response

Phoenixville's 20-25 rate reflects $102,000 median income, 6.3% poverty, and $370,000 home values. Pottstown's 45.8 rate reflects $61,000 income, 17.5% poverty, and $195,000 home values. Crime rates correlate with socioeconomic conditions across all four peer boroughs. Pottstown's rate has fallen 30.5% on the lowest per-resident police budget among peers.

Critique 6: "The department is budgeted for 48. Why can't they get there?"

"If we've approved 48 positions, why are we only at 46?"

Response

Recruiting and retaining sworn officers is a statewide challenge. The department has never been able to reach 48. Currently at 46, with 6 assigned per squad but only 4 working per shift after time off, FMLA, and sick leave. CFS volume is up 21.2% (24,118 to 29,220). Each officer handled 635 calls in 2025 vs. 524 in 2015. Two additional officers would improve working shift strength and reduce per-officer dispatch load from ~10 to ~8 (est.).

Critique 7: "90% of calls are non-crime. Hire civilians, cut the force."

"If nine out of ten calls have nothing to do with crime, why are we sending armed officers?"

Response

"Non-crime" is a UCR classification describing the outcome, not the response requirement at dispatch. The majority of non-crime calls carry legal mandates or immediate safety risks requiring sworn officer response:

Strategic Recommendations

Five data-driven actions for the next 3-5 years
Front-Porch Summary
These five recommendations follow directly from the data. Reaching the budgeted 48-officer strength would increase working shift strength and reduce per-officer call volume.
1

Reach Budgeted Staffing (48 Officers)

The department is budgeted for 48 but has never reached that number. Currently at 46. Each additional officer improves working shift strength beyond the current 4 per shift. Recruiting and retention strategies are the immediate priority.

2

Motor Vehicle Theft Mitigation

MV theft is the one Part I category that increased (+112.3%). 2026 data shows correction underway (annualized ~65 vs. 121 in 2025). Continue manufacturer recall/patch compliance, targeted awareness campaigns, and regional task force coordination.

3

Domestic Violence Investment

Domestic disturbances at 976 (lowest since 1999) but still represent 3.3% of all CFS and are among the most resource-intensive calls. Continue crisis intervention, victim services, and housing stability programs.

4

Proactive Enforcement Restoration

Traffic citations down 56.5% (3,438 to 1,496). DUI arrests down 83.2% (107 to 18). May 2026 data shows traffic citations at 126 vs. 89 in May 2025 (+41.6%).

5

Continue Economic Development

Crime rates and economic indicators correlate across all peer boroughs. Downtown occupancy, housing investment, commercial corridor development, and income growth are the structural levers that produce lasting crime reduction. Phoenixville's trajectory is consistent with this pattern.
